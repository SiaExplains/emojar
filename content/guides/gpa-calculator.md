Your GPA is a credit-weighted average of grade points. Each course's letter grade becomes a number (A = 4.0, B+ = 3.3 and so on), that number is multiplied by the course's credit hours, and the total is divided by all the credits you took. Your cumulative GPA works the same way across every semester, which is why it moves more slowly the further you are into your degree.

## How are letter grades turned into points?

Most US colleges use a 4.0 scale with plus and minus steps. This is the scale the [GPA Calculator](/online-tool/calculators/gpa-calculator) uses:

| Grade | Points | Grade | Points |
| ----- | ------ | ----- | ------ |
| A+    | 4.0    | C+    | 2.3    |
| A     | 4.0    | C     | 2.0    |
| A-    | 3.7    | C-    | 1.7    |
| B+    | 3.3    | D+    | 1.3    |
| B     | 3.0    | D     | 1.0    |
| B-    | 2.7    | D-    | 0.7    |
| F     | 0.0    |       |        |

Schools vary. Some give A+ a 4.3, some skip plus and minus grades entirely, and some don't count pass/fail courses. Check your registrar's scale before relying on a result to the second decimal.

## A worked semester example

Say your semester looks like this:

| Course             | Grade | Credits | Grade points   |
| ------------------ | ----- | ------- | -------------- |
| Calculus           | A-    | 4       | 3.7 × 4 = 14.8 |
| English Literature | B+    | 3       | 3.3 × 3 = 9.9  |
| Biology            | A     | 4       | 4.0 × 4 = 16.0 |
| Elective           | B     | 3       | 3.0 × 3 = 9.0  |
| **Total**          |       | **14**  | **49.7**       |

Semester GPA = 49.7 ÷ 14 = **3.55**.

Notice that a plain average of the four grade values would be 3.5. The weighted figure is higher because the two A-range grades came in 4-credit courses.

## Why credit hours matter so much

Credits decide how much each grade pulls on the average. Compare two students who each earn one A and one C:

- **A in a 4-credit course, C in a 1-credit lab:** (16 + 2) ÷ 5 = **3.6**
- **A in a 1-credit lab, C in a 4-credit course:** (4 + 8) ÷ 5 = **2.4**

Same letters, more than a full point apart. When you're deciding where to put your effort in a hard semester, the heavy courses matter most.

## How is cumulative GPA calculated?

Cumulative GPA treats all your semesters as one long list of courses. The shortcut is to convert your current GPA back into grade points:

1. Previous grade points = current cumulative GPA × credits completed.
2. Add this semester's grade points and credits.
3. Divide total points by total credits.

A student with a 3.2 GPA over 45 credits has 144 grade points. Adding the semester above gives (144 + 49.7) ÷ (45 + 14) = 193.7 ÷ 59 = **3.28**.

## How much can one semester raise your GPA?

This is the question most people really have, and the answer depends on how many credits are already on your record. Here is what happens if a student with a 3.0 earns straight A's across 15 new credits:

| Credits already completed | New cumulative GPA |
| ------------------------- | ------------------ |
| 15                        | 3.50               |
| 60                        | 3.20               |
| 90                        | 3.14               |

A perfect semester moves a first-year's GPA by half a point but a senior's by only about 0.14. The lesson is to protect your GPA early, and to set realistic targets later on.

To find the semester GPA you need for a target, rearrange the formula:

**Needed semester GPA = (target × total credits − current GPA × completed credits) ÷ new credits**

Example: you have a 2.8 over 45 credits and want a 3.0 after a 15-credit semester. That's (3.0 × 60 − 2.8 × 45) ÷ 15 = (180 − 126) ÷ 15 = **3.6**, roughly an A- average. If the result comes out above 4.0, the target isn't reachable in one semester on this scale.

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing quarter and semester credits.** If your school switched systems or you transferred, convert credits first.
- **Including courses that don't count.** Pass/fail, withdrawn and some transfer courses usually carry no grade points.
- **Forgetting retake policies.** Many schools replace the old grade when you retake a course instead of averaging both. In that case, the calculator will understate your new GPA if you simply add the retake as an extra course.

## FAQ

### Is a weighted high school GPA the same thing?

No. High school "weighted" GPAs add bonus points for honors or AP classes, often on a 5.0 scale. This calculator weights by credit hours, which is how college GPAs work.

### Why does my transcript show a slightly different number?

Some registrars truncate rather than round (3.549 becomes 3.54), and they may exclude some courses. Small differences are normal.

### What's a good GPA?

It depends on the goal. Many graduate programs and employers use 3.0 as a soft cutoff, while competitive programs look for 3.5 or higher.
