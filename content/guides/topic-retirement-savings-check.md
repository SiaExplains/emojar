To check whether your retirement savings are on track, you need four numbers: how many years you have until you stop working, the return your money has actually earned, what your pot will be worth at retirement, and how long the income you want will last after that. Four free calculators on Emojar give you each one, and they run in your browser, so your figures stay on your device. The example below follows one person from start to finish.

The calculators only do the arithmetic. They leave out taxes, fees and pensions, so treat the results as estimates.

## How many years do I have until retirement?

Start with the [Age Calculator](/online-tool/calculators/age-calculator). In our example you were born on 9 March 1984. Enter that as the **Date of birth** and today, 17 November 2026, as **Age on**, and the tool says you are 42 years, 8 months, 8 days old, or 15,593 days. It also says your next birthday is in 112 days, on Tuesday, March 9, 2027.

Now change **Age on** to 9 March 2051. The result reads 67 years, 0 months, 0 days, so that is the date you reach 67, which is the retirement age we'll plan for. It's 25 years away. You can round your age down to 42 for the next step, because the retirement calculator works in whole years.

## Is my 6% return assumption realistic?

The retirement calculator defaults to a 6% return before retirement. Check that number against money you already have invested before you trust it. Say you put 20,000 into a fund five years ago and it's worth 26,500 today.

In the [ROI Calculator](/online-tool/calculators/roi-calculator), enter 20,000 as **Amount invested** and 26,500 as **Amount returned (final value)**. Leave **Investment period** on **Number of years** and set **Years** to 5. You get:

- ROI: 32.5%
- Gain: 6,500.00
- Multiple: 1.325×
- Annualized ROI (CAGR): 5.78965%

That 32.5% was earned over five years, so compare the annualized figure, a little under 5.8% a year, with the 6% assumption. It's close enough to keep 6% as the base case here, with 5% as a cautious case. If you know the exact purchase date, switch **Investment period** to **Start and end date** instead. Fees, taxes and dividends count too, so add them to the final value if you want the true return.

In today's money it's worth about 417,000, and the 4% rule supports about 16,700 a year at today's prices. That's a little over half the 30,000 you want.

## When does the money run out?

The withdrawal check answers a different question from the 4% figure. It takes your 30,000, raises it with inflation every year, withdraws it, and lets the rest earn the 4% return in retirement. The **Balance by age** table below the results shows each year. With these inputs the balance peaks at 772,993.96 at age 67, falls to 501,096.31 at 75 and 84,548.97 at 82, and is gone by 84. The summary line puts it at "Runs out around age 83", seven years short of the plan.

Remember the income field means money from these savings only. If you expect a pension or state benefit, subtract it from the 30,000 first.

## How much difference does saving more or retiring later make?

Change one field at a time and watch the summary line:

| Change | Savings at retirement | Wanted income |
| --- | --- | --- |
| None (600 a month, retire at 67) | 772,993.96 | Runs out around age 83 |
| 800 a month | 911,592.75 | Runs out around age 86 |
| 1,100 a month | 1,119,490.94 | Lasts beyond age 90 |
| Retire at 70 | 948,628.48 | Runs out around age 88 |
| 800 a month and retire at 69 | 1,047,856.25 | Lasts beyond age 90 |
| 5% return before retirement | 635,809.06 | Runs out around age 79 |

The last row is the cautious case from the ROI check. With a 5% return the money runs out around 79, four years earlier than the base case. Raising contributions to 1,100 a month gets you to 90. So does a smaller rise to 800 a month combined with two more working years.

## How do I compare two scenarios in percentage terms?

The [Percentage Difference Calculator](/online-tool/calculators/percentage-difference-calculator) turns those pots into percentages. Put the base case, 772,993.96, in **Value A (first / original)** and the 800-a-month result, 911,592.75, in **Value B (second / new)**, with **Decimal places** set to 1:

- Absolute difference |B − A|: 138,598.8
- Percentage change A → B: +17.9% (increase)
- Percentage difference (symmetric): 16.5%

So 200 more a month gives you a pot 17.9% bigger. Swap Value B for the 5% case, 635,809.06, and the change is −17.7%. A return one point lower takes away about as much as the extra 200 a month adds. Use **Percentage change** when one number is your starting point, as here. **Percentage difference** compares the two against their average and comes out the same whichever way round you enter them.

## FAQ

### Does the retirement calculator include pensions or state benefits?

No. Take what you expect from pensions or state benefits away from the yearly income you want, and enter only the part your savings need to cover.

### Why are there two income figures?

"Income at 4 % withdrawal" is a percentage of the pot at retirement, shown in future money and in today's money. The "Wanted income" line runs your own figure year by year, raised with inflation each year, and tells you the age when the money runs out or that it lasts beyond the plan-until age.

### What's the difference between ROI and annualized ROI?

ROI is the total gain divided by what you invested, however long it took. Annualized ROI (CAGR) spreads that gain into a steady yearly rate, which is the figure to compare with the return you assume in the retirement calculator.

### Are my numbers saved or sent anywhere?

No. The calculations run in your browser, and nothing you type is uploaded.
