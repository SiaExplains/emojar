The Pomodoro Technique is simple: work on one task for 25 minutes, take a 5-minute break, and after four rounds take a longer break of 15 to 30 minutes. The timer does the clock-watching for you. What makes it work, or not, is what you do around the timer: picking the task before you start, protecting the 25 minutes from interruptions, and actually resting during breaks.

## How a full Pomodoro cycle adds up

With the standard settings, one complete set of four rounds looks like this:

| Block       | Length | Running total |
| ----------- | ------ | ------------- |
| Focus 1     | 25 min | 0:25          |
| Short break | 5 min  | 0:30          |
| Focus 2     | 25 min | 0:55          |
| Short break | 5 min  | 1:00          |
| Focus 3     | 25 min | 1:25          |
| Short break | 5 min  | 1:30          |
| Focus 4     | 25 min | 1:55          |
| Long break  | 15 min | 2:10          |

That's **100 minutes of focused work in 2 hours 10 minutes**. A typical working day has room for two or three of these sets, which is a useful reality check. For many people, six to ten genuinely focused Pomodoros adds up to a productive day, not a lazy one.

## Before you start: a 2-minute checklist

- **Pick one task and write it down.** "Draft the introduction" works. "Work on the report" is too vague to finish in 25 minutes.
- **Break big tasks into Pomodoro-sized pieces.** If something will take more than four or five rounds, split it.
- **Close what will interrupt you.** Silence your phone, close the email tab, and set chat to "busy".
- **Have a capture list ready.** Paper or a text file for the thoughts that pop up ("reply to Sam", "buy milk"), so you can note them and keep going.
- **Allow notifications for the timer tab.** That way the end of a phase reaches you while you work in another window.

## During a focus session

- **If you get distracted internally,** write the thought on the capture list and return to the task. Don't act on it.
- **If someone interrupts you,** the technique's classic advice is "inform, negotiate, call back": tell them you're in the middle of something, agree a time, and get back to it.
- **If you finish early,** use the remaining minutes to review or polish the work rather than starting something new.
- **If a session is truly broken,** with a phone call or an urgent problem, reset and start that round again. A Pomodoro is meant to be indivisible.

## During breaks

The break is part of the method, not a reward you can skip.

- **Stand up and move.** Get water, stretch, look at something far away.
- **Avoid "quick checks".** Email and social media pull your attention back into work mode, or worse, into someone else's work.
- **Use the long break properly.** After four rounds, step away for real: a walk, a snack, or a chat with someone.

## After each set: a quick review

Note how many Pomodoros the task took versus your guess. Over a week or two, that comparison improves your estimates more than almost anything else.

## When should you change the 25/5 rhythm?

The 25-minute default is a good starting point, especially for tasks you're avoiding. Starting is easier when the commitment is short. But it's not sacred:

- **Deep work** like coding, writing or design often benefits from **50/10**. Getting back into a complex problem takes a few minutes, and 25-minute blocks can cut you off just as you settle in.
- **Very hard or unpleasant tasks** can start with **15/5** to build momentum.
- **Studying** often works well at the default, with the long break used for a short self-quiz.

Experiment for a few days with one setting before switching. Your attention changes over a day, so the right length at 9 a.m. may not be right at 4 p.m.

## FAQ

### What if I'm in flow when the timer rings?

Many people finish the current sentence or thought, then take the break anyway. If you regularly hate stopping, that's a sign to try longer focus blocks, not to skip breaks.

### Does the Pomodoro Technique work for meetings or calls?

Not really. It's designed for focused solo work. Use it for the work between meetings instead.

### Who invented it?

Francesco Cirillo, in the late 1980s, while a university student. He named it after the tomato-shaped kitchen timer he used. "Pomodoro" is Italian for tomato.
