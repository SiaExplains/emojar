Miles per gallon measures distance per unit of fuel, but what you pay for is fuel per unit of distance. Because those two are reciprocals, equal-looking mpg gains don't save equal amounts of fuel. **Going from 10 to 12 mpg saves more fuel than going from 40 to 50 mpg.** Fuel-per-distance units such as **L/100 km** or **gallons per 100 miles** show the savings directly, which is why they are the better way to compare upgrades.

Researchers call this the "MPG illusion". Here is a worked example with real numbers.

## A worked comparison: which upgrade saves the most fuel?

Imagine a household with three vehicles, each driven 10,000 miles a year. They can afford to upgrade one:

- **Option A:** replace an old pickup getting 10 mpg with one getting 12 mpg.
- **Option B:** replace a sedan getting 20 mpg with one getting 25 mpg.
- **Option C:** replace a hybrid getting 40 mpg with one getting 50 mpg.

On paper, Option C looks best because it's the biggest jump at +10 mpg. Option A looks feeble at +2 mpg. Now divide 10,000 miles by each mpg figure to see how much fuel is actually burned:

| Upgrade        | Fuel before | Fuel after | Saved per year |
| -------------- | ----------- | ---------- | -------------- |
| A: 10 → 12 mpg | 1,000 gal   | 833 gal    | **167 gal**    |
| B: 20 → 25 mpg | 500 gal     | 400 gal    | **100 gal**    |
| C: 40 → 50 mpg | 250 gal     | 200 gal    | **50 gal**     |

The "small" pickup upgrade saves more than three times as much fuel as the hybrid upgrade.

## The same comparison in L/100 km

Convert each figure to liters per 100 km and the ranking is obvious straight away, with no division needed:

| US mpg | L/100 km | gal/100 mi |
| ------ | -------- | ---------- |
| 10     | 23.52    | 10         |
| 12     | 19.60    | 8.33       |
| 20     | 11.76    | 5          |
| 25     | 9.41     | 4          |
| 40     | 5.88     | 2.5        |
| 50     | 4.70     | 2          |

Option A cuts consumption by 3.92 L/100 km, Option B by 2.35 and Option C by 1.18. Fuel-per-distance numbers can be subtracted directly, and the differences scale with what you spend at the pump. That's why most of the world uses them, and why US window stickers now print gallons per 100 miles next to mpg.

## Percentages can mislead too

A "20% better mpg" doesn't mean 20% less fuel. Going from 10 to 12 mpg is a 20% increase in mpg but a 16.7% reduction in fuel used. The effect also runs the other way. A car that drops from **8 to 6 L/100 km** uses 25% less fuel, yet its rating climbs from 29.4 to 39.2 US mpg, which is a 33% increase. When you compare claims, check which kind of percentage you're reading.

## US mpg, UK mpg and why the same car has two ratings

An imperial gallon (4.546 L) is about 20% bigger than a US gallon (3.785 L). So the same car posts a higher mpg in Britain:

- **30 US mpg** is 36.0 UK mpg, or 7.84 L/100 km.
- **40 UK mpg** is only 33.3 US mpg.
- **5 L/100 km** is 47.0 US mpg or 56.5 UK mpg.

If you're comparing a UK review with a US one, convert both to L/100 km first, because liters are the same everywhere.

## When to use which unit

- **Comparing cars or upgrades:** use L/100 km or gal/100 mi. Differences between them translate directly into fuel and money.
- **Estimating range from a full tank:** use mpg or km/L. Multiply by the tank size to get distance.
- **Reading foreign reviews:** convert everything to L/100 km first, so US and UK gallons can't get mixed up.

## FAQ

### Does a higher L/100 km mean a better car?

No, it's the opposite. L/100 km is fuel used, so lower is better. A 4.7 L/100 km car is more efficient than a 9.4 L/100 km one.

### How do I convert km/L to L/100 km?

Divide 100 by the km/L figure. 15 km/L is 6.67 L/100 km, or about 35.3 US mpg.

### Why not just compare mpg differences?

Because equal mpg steps save less and less fuel as mpg rises. Only fuel-per-distance differences line up with the fuel you actually buy.
