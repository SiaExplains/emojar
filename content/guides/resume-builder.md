A good resume fits on one page for most people with under ten years of experience. It puts your name and contact details at the top, then a short profile, then your jobs in reverse order, with achievements that start with a verb and include a number. You can build one in the [Resume Builder](/online-tool/productivity-tools/resume-builder) and download a text-based PDF that applicant tracking systems can read, without signing up.

## What do recruiters look at first?

Most recruiters skim a resume before they read it. In those first seconds they check four things: your current or most recent job title, the companies, the dates, and whether your skills match the posting. Everything in the layout should make those four easy to find.

That is why simple resumes outperform decorative ones. One clear column of text, consistent dates on the right and bold job titles do more than icons, photos or skill bars.

## Section by section

**Header.** Your full name, a headline that matches the job you want ("Product Designer", "Senior Accountant"), an email address, a phone number, your city and one or two links, such as LinkedIn or a portfolio. You don't need a full street address.

**Profile.** Two to four sentences: who you are, your strongest skills and what you want next. Skip clichés like "hard-working team player". A good example: "Product designer with 7 years of experience turning research into simple, accessible interfaces. I run usability tests every sprint and care about measurable outcomes."

**Experience.** Your most recent job first. For each role give the title, company, location and dates, then three to five bullet points. Older or less relevant jobs can have one or two bullets, or none.

**Education.** Your degree, school and years. Recent graduates can add honours or relevant courses; after a few years of work, one line is enough.

**Skills and languages.** A comma-separated list of tools and skills that appear in the job posting and that you can actually discuss in an interview. Languages with an honest level, such as "German (B2)" or "Spanish (conversational)".

**Projects** are optional. They are useful for career changers and students, or when your best work happened outside a job.

## How to write achievement bullets

Weak bullets describe duties. Strong bullets describe results. A reliable pattern is **verb + what you did + measurable result**:

- Weak: "Responsible for the booking flow."
- Strong: "Redesigned the patient booking flow, cutting drop-off by 23%."
- Weak: "Worked on a design system."
- Strong: "Built and documented a component library used by 4 product teams."

If you don't have exact numbers, use honest estimates or scale: how many users, how much time saved, how many people you trained. Start each bullet with a different verb when you can, such as led, built, cut, launched or introduced.

## How do applicant tracking systems read a resume?

Many companies pass resumes through an applicant tracking system (ATS) before a person sees them. The software extracts your name, contact details, job titles, dates and skills into a database, and recruiters then search or filter it.

To be read correctly, a resume needs:

- **Real text, not an image.** A resume exported as a picture, or a scan, gives the system nothing to read. The PDF from the Resume Builder contains real, selectable text.
- **Standard headings.** "Experience", "Education" and "Skills" are what parsers expect. Creative headings like "My Journey" can confuse them.
- **One reading order.** Tables, text boxes and multiple columns can come out scrambled. The Classic template is a single column; the Modern template keeps contact details and skills in a sidebar, which most current systems handle well. If an application portal is known to be strict, use Classic.
- **Keywords from the posting.** If the job asks for "financial modelling" and "Excel", use those exact words where they are true for you.

## Practical tips

- **Tailor, don't rewrite.** Keep one master resume and adjust the headline, profile and top bullets for each application. Use **Export JSON** to keep a backup of each version.
- **Check the length of your text.** The [Word Counter](/online-tool/text-tools/word-counter) helps you keep the profile under about 60 words.
- **Use a professional photo only where it is expected.** In the US and UK resumes usually have no photo; in parts of Europe one is common. If you need one, the [Profile Picture Maker](/online-tool/image-tools/profile-picture-maker) crops it neatly.
- **Match your email signature.** A consistent name and title across your resume and emails looks polished; the [Email Signature Generator](/online-tool/security-generators/email-signature-generator) builds one in a minute.

Your resume is saved only in your browser, so nothing you type is uploaded. Export a JSON backup before you clear your browser data or switch computers.
