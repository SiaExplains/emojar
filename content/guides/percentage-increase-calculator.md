Percentage increase is the change divided by the **original** value, times 100: going from 80 to 100 is (100 − 80) ÷ 80 × 100 = 25%. Most mistakes with percentage change come from forgetting that the original value, the base, is different every time you move. Fix the base and the myths below fall apart.

## The formula in one line

**Percentage change = (new − original) ÷ original × 100.**

A positive result is an increase, a negative one a decrease. To go the other way and apply a percentage to a number, multiply by (1 + p ÷ 100) to increase it or (1 − p ÷ 100) to decrease it.

## Myth 1: "A 25% rise and a 25% fall cancel out"

A price goes from 80 to 100. That is a **25% increase**, because the change of 20 is measured against 80. Now the price falls back from 100 to 80. The change is still 20, but it is measured against 100, so it is only a **20% decrease**.

Same distance, different base. The direction you travel decides which number sits underneath the fraction.

## Myth 2: "Up 50%, then down 50%, means no change"

Start with 100 and increase it by 50%: you have 150. Decrease 150 by 50% and you land on **75**, not 100. The 50% cut takes away 75, which is half of the bigger number.

Smaller swings behave the same way. Up 10% then down 10% turns 100 into 110 and then into **99**. This is why an investment that loses 50% needs to gain 100% to recover.

## Myth 3: "A rate going from 4% to 5% rose by 1%"

It rose by **1 percentage point**. As a percentage of the original rate, it rose by 25%: (5 − 4) ÷ 4 × 100 = 25%. Both statements are true, but they answer different questions. News reports about interest rates, unemployment and polls should say "points" when they mean the simple difference. If they don't, check which one they mean before drawing conclusions.

## Myth 4: "Anything can drop by more than 100%"

A positive quantity can rise by any percentage, but it can only fall by 100% at most, because a 100% drop takes it to zero. A share price can triple (a 200% increase, for example 20 → 60), but it cannot fall by 150%. If you see "costs dropped by 300%", someone has flipped the base, usually by dividing the change by the new, smaller number.

## Myth 5: "You can reverse a percentage with the same percentage"

If a shop adds 15% to a price of 80, you pay **92**. To get back to 80 from 92 you divide by 1.15; you don't subtract 15%. Subtracting 15% of 92 gives 78.20, which is wrong. ## When percentage change is the wrong tool

- **Very small bases.** Going from 2 customers to 6 is a 200% increase, which sounds dramatic but is just four people. Report the absolute change alongside the percentage.
- **Multi-year growth.** Three years of 10% growth is not 30%; it compounds to 33.1% (1.1 × 1.1 × 1.1 = 1.331). For growth over time, the [Compound Interest Calculator](/online-tool/calculators/compound-interest-calculator) is a better fit.
- **Comparing rates.** Use percentage points for the gap between two percentages.

## FAQ

### What is the difference between percentage increase and percentage difference?

Percentage increase has a direction and a fixed base: the original value. "Percentage difference" is often used for two values with no natural starting point and divides by their average instead. This calculator always measures against the original value you enter.

### How do I find the original value before an increase?

Divide the new value by (1 + p ÷ 100). If a price is 92 after a 15% increase, the original was 92 ÷ 1.15 = 80.

### Why does the result say "decrease" when I typed a bigger percentage?

The label follows the direction of the change, not the size of the number. Whenever the new value is smaller than the original, the result is a decrease.
