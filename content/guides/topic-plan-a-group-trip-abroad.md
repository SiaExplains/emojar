To plan a group trip abroad without arguing about it for weeks, score the candidate cities in the [Decision Matrix](/online-tool/productivity-tools/decision-matrix), convert the forecast and the distances with the [Celsius to Fahrenheit Converter](/online-tool/unit-converters/temperature-converter/celsius-to-fahrenheit) and the [Kilometers to Miles Converter](/online-tool/unit-converters/length-converter/km-to-miles), split each restaurant bill with the [Tip Calculator](/online-tool/calculators/tip-calculator), and set the [Online Alarm Clock](/online-tool/date-time-tools/alarm-clock) for the morning you fly. All five run in your browser. Nothing you type is uploaded, and the matrix and the alarms are saved only in that browser.

The example follows four friends planning a long weekend in the US in October. They have three cities on the list, Chicago, Boston and Nashville, and no agreement on which one to pick.

## How do you choose a city when everyone wants something different?

Open the Decision Matrix and type the question into **Decision**, for example "Which city for our October weekend?". The columns are options and the rows are criteria. Rename the two starting options and use **+ Add option** for the third, then add criteria with **+ Add criterion**. The tool takes up to 12 options and 30 criteria.

The group settles on four criteria and agrees on the weights before anyone scores, which the tool's own notes recommend so nobody can tune the weights until their favourite wins:

| Criterion | Weight | Lower is better | Chicago | Boston | Nashville |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Flight cost | 35% | yes | 6 | 8 | 4 |
| Things to do | 30% | no | 8 | 7 | 7 |
| October weather | 20% | no | 6 | 6 | 8 |
| Getting around without a car | 15% | no | 9 | 8 | 4 |

Leave **Score scale** on **1 to 10** and **Enter weights as** on **Percentages (total 100)**. The weights above add up to 100, so no warning appears. If yours don't, the results still use each weight's share of the total, and **Normalise to 100%** rescales them.

Flight cost is rated as an amount, so a pricier flight gets a higher number. Tick **Lower is better** on that row and the score is inverted on the scale: on 1 to 10 the new score is 11 minus the old one, so Chicago's 6 counts as 5, Boston's 8 as 3 and Nashville's 4 as 7.

Each total is a weighted average out of 10. Chicago gets 0.35 × 5 + 0.30 × 8 + 0.20 × 6 + 0.15 × 9 = 6.7. Boston gets 5.55 and Nashville 6.75. The summary line reads "Nashville wins with 6.75 / 10, 0.05 points (0.7%) ahead of Chicago."

## What should you do when two options are almost tied?

A lead of 0.05 on a 10-point scale doesn't settle much. The tool's advice for a gap of a few tenths is to call it a tie and look at the criterion where the two options differ most. Here that is getting around without a car: Chicago scored 9 and Nashville 4. Nobody in the group wants to rent a car or pay for taxis all weekend, so they pick Chicago and leave the weights alone.

To keep a record, **Copy table** copies tab-separated values that paste into a spreadsheet, **Download CSV** saves a file, and **🖨 Print** prints the matrix. With three or more criteria, **Show radar chart** draws each city as a shape, which makes the gap on the car question easy to see.

## How do you read a forecast in Fahrenheit and distances in miles?

US weather apps and signs use °F and miles. Open the Celsius to Fahrenheit Converter, press **⇄** (Swap units) so it converts from °F to °C, and type the forecast. The formula is °F = °C × 9/5 + 32. A high of 59 °F is 15 °C and a low of 41 °F is 5 °C, so everyone packs a warm jacket. Under the result, a table shows the same temperature in every unit the page supports, including Kelvin and Rankine.

The Kilometers to Miles Converter works the same way. One kilometer is 0.621371 miles and a mile is exactly 1.609344 km. A bike tour listed as 12 miles comes out at 19.312128 km after a swap. The copy button next to the result puts the number on your clipboard without the thousands separators.

## How much should you tip, and how do you split the bill?

The Tip Calculator's notes give customary tips of around 15–20% in the US, 5–10% or rounding up in much of Europe, and none in Japan. The group settles on 20% at sit-down dinners.

Their first dinner comes to $184.60 before the tip. Enter that as **Bill amount**, set **Tip** to 20 and **Split between** to 4. The results:

- **Total per person:** 55.38
- **Tip amount:** 36.92
- **Total bill with tip:** 221.52
- **Tip per person:** 9.23

The calculator rounds the number of people to a whole number and never goes below 1. It has no currency setting, so the amounts are plain numbers in whatever currency the bill uses. On tax, the tool's FAQ says etiquette guides usually suggest tipping on the pre-tax amount, though many people tip on the total. If the receipt shows tax separately, enter the pre-tax figure.

## How do you make sure nobody sleeps through an early flight?

The flight home leaves at 7:10 a.m., and the group wants to be out of the hotel by 5:15. Open the Online Alarm Clock on a laptop the night before and click **▶ Test sound** once. Browsers block audio until you interact with the page, and that one click unlocks it.

Pick 4:30 as the time and type a label such as "Airport, leave 5:15" in **Label (optional)**. Under **Sound**, choose from **Classic beep**, **Digital alarm**, **Chime** and **Gentle rise**. Leave **Repeat (leave empty for once)** empty so it rings once, then click **Add alarm**. A one-time alarm switches itself off after it rings. Tick **Also show a notification** if the tab will sit in the background.

When it goes off, press **Stop**, or **Snooze 5 min** or **Snooze 10 min**. The big live clock on the page shows how long is left until the next alarm, so you can check the time is right before you go to sleep.

The alarm rings only while the tab is open and the device is awake, and a laptop that goes to sleep pauses the browser. Keep it plugged in with the lid open, and set your phone's built-in alarm as well for anything as important as a flight.

## FAQ

### Do the weights in the Decision Matrix have to add up to 100?

No. The totals use each weight's share of the sum, so 35, 30, 20 and 15 give the same result as 7, 6, 4 and 3. In percentage mode the tool warns when the total isn't 100, and "Normalise to 100%" rescales the weights for you.

### What does "lower is better" do to a score?

It inverts the score on the scale you chose. On 1 to 10, a 2 counts as 9 and a 9 counts as 2. Use it for anything rated as an amount where less is better, such as flight cost or travel time.

### Does the Tip Calculator work with euros or pounds?

Yes. It has no currency setting and shows plain numbers with two decimals, so enter the bill in any currency and read the results in the same one.

### Will the online alarm ring if my laptop goes to sleep?

No. A sleeping device pauses the browser, so the alarm can't ring. Leave the tab open, keep the laptop awake and plugged in, and use your phone's alarm as a backup for an early flight.
