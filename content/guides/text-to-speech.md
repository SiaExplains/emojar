Text to speech is most useful for three things: catching mistakes in your own writing, getting through long reading while your eyes are busy or tired, and hearing how a language or a script actually sounds. A browser-based reader does all three with the voices already on your device. Paste text, choose a voice, set the speed, and press Play.

Here are eight ways to put it to work, with the settings that suit each.

## What powers browser text to speech?

The [Text to Speech](/online-tool/text-tools/text-to-speech) tool uses the Web Speech API built into modern browsers. The voices come from your operating system and browser, so the list differs between a Windows laptop, a Mac and an Android phone. On-device voices are listed first and work offline. Voices marked **online** are provided by your browser vendor, and the text is sent to their speech service when you use one; the tool shows a notice when you pick one. If your text is private, choose a voice without the "online" label.

Two sliders shape the sound: **Speed** from 0.5× to 2.0× and **Pitch** from 0.5 to 2.0, both starting at 1.0.

## 1. Proofread your writing by ear

Your eyes autocorrect what you meant to write; your ears don't. Hearing a sentence read flatly by a synthetic voice exposes missing words, doubled words ("the the"), clumsy rhythm and sentences that run out of breath.

**Settings:** Speed 1.0× or slightly below. With most voices, each word is highlighted in the text as it's spoken, so you can see exactly where a problem is. Press Stop to edit, then Play again.

## 2. Listen to long articles instead of reading them

Paste a long report or article and listen while you tidy your desk or rest your eyes. The tool shows a word count and an estimated listening time, based on about 150 words per minute at 1.0×, adjusted for your chosen speed:

| Text length | At 1.0×      | At 1.5×      | At 2.0×      |
| ----------- | ------------ | ------------ | ------------ |
| 600 words   | about 4 min  | about 3 min  | about 2 min  |
| 1,200 words | about 8 min  | about 5 min  | about 4 min  |
| 3,000 words | about 20 min | about 13 min | about 10 min |

Most people adapt to 1.3–1.5× quickly once they're used to a voice.

## 3. Practice pronunciation in another language

Pick a voice in the language you're learning; the menu shows each voice's language code, such as `es-ES` or `fr-FR`. Then paste a dialogue from your textbook.

**Settings:** Speed 0.7–0.8× to hear each syllable, then back to 1.0× to train your ear for natural pace. Note that a voice reads in its own language rules. An English voice reading Spanish text will mangle it, so always match the voice to the text.

## 4. Time a speech or presentation

Paste your talk and play it at 1.0×. A synthetic voice reads at a steady pace without the pauses you'll naturally add, so treat the result as a **minimum**. If your 5-minute slot plays back at 5 minutes, it will run long in person. Aim for about 80–85% of the slot.

## 5. Read text trapped in a PDF or image

Text to speech needs plain text. If what you want to hear is inside a document or a photo, extract it first:

- From a PDF with a text layer, use [PDF to Text](/online-tool/pdf-tools/pdf-to-text).
- From a screenshot, photo or scan, use [Image to Text](/online-tool/image-tools/image-to-text).

Then paste the result here. Quickly scan it for OCR errors first, since a misread word sounds stranger than it looks.

## 6. Give your eyes a break

After a long day of screens, listening is less tiring than reading. It's also helpful for people with dyslexia or low vision who find long passages hard going. Pick the clearest voice you have, as some sound noticeably more natural than others, and keep the pitch at 1.0, since large pitch changes reduce clarity.

## 7. Check how a script sounds before recording

Writing for a video, podcast or voicemail greeting? Hearing it read aloud reveals tongue-twisters and sentences too long to say in one breath. Rewrite until the synthetic version flows; a human reading will flow even better.

The browser plays the speech but doesn't give web pages access to the audio, so this is for listening, not for exporting a voiceover file.

## 8. Hear your text the way a listener will

Instructions, recipes and announcements are often heard rather than read. Play them back to check that they make sense without visual cues like bullet points and headings. Lists that look tidy on screen can sound like a jumble when spoken.

## How to use the tool step by step

1. Open [Text to Speech](/online-tool/text-tools/text-to-speech) and paste your text into **Text to read** (a sample is pre-filled).
2. Choose a **Voice**. Each entry shows its language, and online voices are labeled.
3. Adjust **Speed** and **Pitch**.
4. Press **Play**. Use **Pause**, **Resume** and **Stop** as needed, and follow the highlighted word as it's read (if your voice reports word positions).

## FAQ

### Why do I see different voices on my phone and my laptop?

The voices belong to each device's operating system and browser, not to the website. Installing extra voices in your system's language or accessibility settings adds them to the list.

### Why does one voice sound robotic and another natural?

Voice quality varies a lot between engines. Older on-device voices tend to sound mechanical, while newer or "enhanced" voices (and the online ones) sound smoother. Try a few from the list with the same sentence.

### Can I edit the text while it's reading?

While speech is playing, the text is shown read-only with the highlight. Press Stop to return to editing.
