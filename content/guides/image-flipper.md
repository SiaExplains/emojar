Flipping an image horizontally mirrors it left to right, like looking in a mirror; flipping vertically mirrors it top to bottom, like a reflection in a lake. Neither is the same as rotating: rotation turns the picture without reversing it, while a flip reverses it, which is why text in a flipped image reads backwards. Choose a flip when something is mirrored, and a rotation when something is merely turned.

That distinction trips up a lot of people. Here are five myths worth clearing up.

## Myth 1: "Flipping vertically is the same as rotating 180°"

Both turn the picture upside down, so they look similar at a glance. Look at any text, though. Rotate the word "PIZZA" by 180° and it's upside down but still reads correctly if you turn your head. Flip it vertically and it's upside down _and_ mirrored, so even turning your head won't fix it.

The surprising part is that **a horizontal flip plus a vertical flip equals a 180° rotation.** Mirroring twice, along two perpendicular axes, cancels the "reversed" effect and leaves just a half turn. You can check this in the flipper: tick both boxes and the preview matches a 180° rotation exactly.

## Myth 2: "Selfies are always saved backwards"

Not quite. Front cameras usually show you a mirrored _preview_, because that matches what you see in a bathroom mirror and makes framing feel natural. What gets _saved_ depends on your phone and its settings: some save the mirrored version, some save the un-mirrored "as others see you" version.

The giveaway is text. If the logo on your T-shirt or a street sign reads backwards in the saved photo, it was saved mirrored, and a horizontal flip fixes it.

## Myth 3: "Flipping always loses quality"

A flip doesn't blend or resample anything. Each pixel just moves to its mirror position. In a 1000-pixel-wide image, the pixel at column 100 moves to column 899 (counting from 0: 1000 − 1 − 100), and its color is copied exactly.

What can cost a tiny bit of quality is saving the file again. The flipper keeps JPG and WebP in their original format at a high quality setting (95%), and saves everything else as lossless PNG. For practical purposes the result is indistinguishable from the original.

## Myth 4: "Mirroring is harmless for any photo"

Mirroring is perfect for selfies with backwards text, symmetric designs, and making two product shots face each other on a page. It's a bad idea when the image contains:

- **Text, numbers or logos** that viewers will read, unless you're correcting an already-mirrored photo.
- **Recognizable places**, where a mirrored skyline or street will look wrong to locals.
- **Directional details** such as a wristwatch, a wedding ring, car steering wheels or which side people part their hair. Flipped, these quietly look "off."
- **Diagrams and maps**, where left and right carry meaning.

## Myth 5: "A flipped image is a new, original image"

Flipping someone else's photo doesn't make it yours, and it doesn't hide it from reverse image search engines either, which are generally good at spotting simple transformations. Flip images you have the rights to use.

## When does flipping actually help?

- **Fixing mirrored selfies and webcam captures** so text reads correctly.
- **Layout balance**: making a person or product face _into_ the page rather than off its edge.
- **Mock-ups and patterns**: creating symmetric designs by pairing an image with its mirror.
- **Craft and printing**: iron-on transfers and some printing processes need a mirrored image so the final result reads correctly.

## FAQ

### Does flipping change the image's dimensions?

No. A 1920 × 1080 image is still 1920 × 1080 after a horizontal or vertical flip. Only rotations by 90° swap width and height.

### Can I flip an image back?

Yes. Flipping the same direction again restores the original arrangement exactly. Keep the original file anyway, so you avoid an extra save.

### Will the flipped photo keep its camera details and location?

No. Saving through the browser drops EXIF metadata such as camera model and GPS coordinates, which is usually a privacy bonus.
