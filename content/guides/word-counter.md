A word counter treats every unbroken run of letters or numbers as one word, so "don't", "well-known" and "2026" each count once, and spaces, dashes standing alone and emoji count as nothing. Reading time is just the word count divided by an average reading speed. Once you know those two rules, you can predict almost any count before you paste your text in.

That matters more than it sounds. If you're writing to a 500-word limit for a scholarship essay or a 1,500-word brief for a client, a surprise 40-word gap between two tools can be annoying. Here's what is actually being counted.

## What counts as a word?

The [Emojar Word Counter](/online-tool/text-tools/word-counter) uses one rule: a word starts with a letter or digit and continues through letters, digits, apostrophes and hyphens. Everything else is a separator. In practice:

| Text            | Words counted | Why                              |
| --------------- | ------------- | -------------------------------- |
| don't           | 1             | Apostrophes stay inside the word |
| well-known      | 1             | Hyphens join the parts           |
| 2026            | 1             | Numbers are words                |
| 3.5             | 2             | The period splits "3" and "5"    |
| rock — paper    | 2             | A spaced dash is not a word      |
| e-mail vs email | 1 and 1       | Same count either way            |
| 👋 Hi           | 1             | Emoji are not letters or digits  |

The rule works for any alphabet, so "café", "Straße" and "Москва" count as one word each.

## Why do different tools give different counts?

Most disagreements come from a handful of edge cases:

- **Decimals and versions.** "3.5" or "v2.1" can count as one word in one app and two in another.
- **Slashes.** "and/or" is two words here, because the slash is a separator.
- **Spaced dashes and bullets.** Some editors count a lone "–" or "•" as a word; this counter ignores them.
- **Hidden text.** Word processors may or may not include footnotes, headers and text boxes. When you paste into a browser tool, you only get what you copied.

For ordinary prose, these cases are rare, which is why the count usually matches Microsoft Word and Google Docs. If you're close to a hard limit, check the tool your teacher or editor will use and leave yourself a small buffer.

## How is reading time calculated?

Reading time is words divided by 238, the average silent reading speed for adults. Speaking time is words divided by 150, a comfortable presentation pace. A worked example:

- A 1,200-word blog post: 1,200 ÷ 238 ≈ 5.04 minutes, shown as **5 min 3 sec**.
- The same text read aloud: 1,200 ÷ 150 = **8 min**.
- A 400-word wedding toast: 400 ÷ 150 ≈ 2.7 minutes, shown as **2 min 40 sec**.

These are averages, not stopwatches. Dense technical text reads slower, and nervous speakers talk faster. For a timed talk, rehearse once and compare your real time with the estimate, then adjust your word target.

## How to check your word count step by step

1. Open the Word Counter and paste or type your text into the box labeled **Your text**.
2. Read the four tiles at the top: **Words**, **Characters**, **Sentences** and **Paragraphs**. They update as you type.
3. Look under **More details** for characters without spaces, line count, reading time and speaking time.
4. Check **Most used words** to spot repetition. It lists your ten most frequent words, skipping words shorter than three letters and common filler such as "the", "and" and "with".
5. Use **Clear** to start over.

Your text is processed in your browser and isn't uploaded or saved, so it's fine to paste a draft you haven't shared with anyone yet.

## Using "Most used words" to tighten a draft

The frequency list is the most underrated part of the tool. Paste a cover letter and you may see "experience ×7" or "team ×6" at the top. That's a signal to vary your wording or cut sentences that repeat the same point. For SEO writing, it's a quick sanity check that your main topic actually appears in the text, and a warning when one phrase appears so often it reads unnaturally.

## When a word count isn't the right measure

Some limits aren't measured in words at all. Social posts, SMS messages and meta descriptions are limited by characters, so use the [Character Counter](/online-tool/text-tools/character-counter) for those. If your concern is readability rather than length, the [Sentence Counter](/online-tool/text-tools/sentence-counter) shows your average sentence length, which tells you more about how hard a text is to read than the total does.

## FAQ

### Do headings and captions count toward an essay's word count?

That depends on your instructor or publisher, not the tool. The counter counts everything you paste, so if headings or a reference list are excluded from your limit, leave them out when you copy.

### How many pages is 1,000 words?

Roughly two pages single-spaced or four pages double-spaced in a 12-point font with standard margins. Font, spacing and paragraph breaks change this a lot, so treat it as a rough guide.

### Why is my reading time shorter than I expected?

238 words per minute assumes silent reading of everyday prose. If your audience is skimming documentation or reading in a second language, plan for more time than the estimate.
