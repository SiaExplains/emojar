To scale a bread recipe, first turn every ingredient into grams, then write each one as a percentage of the flour weight. Those baker's percentages stay the same at any size, so for a bigger or smaller batch you pick a flour weight and multiply. On Emojar you can do the whole job with five free calculators: the [Cooking Measurement Converter](/online-tool/unit-converters/cooking-measurement-converter) for cups to grams, the [Percentage Calculator](/online-tool/calculators/percentage-calculator) for the percentages, the [Ratio Calculator](/online-tool/calculators/ratio-calculator) for scaling and splitting flour, the [Percentage Increase Calculator](/online-tool/calculators/percentage-increase-calculator) for adjusting the water, and the [Time Duration Calculator](/online-tool/calculators/time-duration-calculator) for the proofing schedule.

The example is a plain white sandwich loaf from an American recipe card: 3 cups of bread flour, 1 cup of water, 1½ teaspoons of table salt and 2¼ teaspoons of instant yeast, baked at 375 °F. The plan is to make it bigger, swap some of the flour for whole wheat and let the dough rise overnight in the fridge.

## How do you convert a cup recipe into grams?

Open the Cooking Measurement Converter. Enter the **Amount**, choose the unit you have under **From** and **Weight: Gram** under **To**, then pick the ingredient. The ingredient matters here because a cup is a volume and a gram is a weight, and every ingredient has a different density. The label above the list changes to "Ingredient (needed to convert volume ↔ weight)" as soon as you convert between the two.

For the loaf:

| Recipe says | Ingredient in the list | Grams |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3 cups | Bread flour — 120 g per cup | 360 |
| 1 cup | Water — 236.6 g per cup | 236.6 |
| 1½ tsp | Table salt — 288 g per cup | 9 |
| 2¼ tsp | Instant / active dry yeast — 149 g per cup | 6.984 |

Use **Volume: Teaspoon (US)** for the salt and yeast. The result is rounded to four significant figures, and a table underneath shows the same amount in other common units. Round the yeast to 7 g; no home scale will tell 6.984 g apart from 7.

The converter's flour figure is for a cup that's spooned in and levelled. A cup scooped straight from the bag holds more, which is one more reason to weigh from now on.

The same page has an oven temperature table at the bottom. Find 375 °F in the °F column and you'll see it's gas mark 5, 190 °C or 170 °C in a fan oven.

## How do you work out baker's percentages?

In baker's percentages the flour is always 100%, and every other ingredient is its weight divided by the flour weight. In the Percentage Calculator, set **Calculation** to **X is what % of Y?**, put the ingredient in **X** and the flour in **Y**.

- Water: 236.6 of 360 gives 65.7222%. Bakers call this the hydration.
- Salt: 9 of 360 gives 2.5%.
- Yeast: 7 of 360 gives 1.94444%.

Write those numbers down, because they work at any batch size. A hydration of about 66% is why this dough is easy to shape. If a recipe you find online lists 80% water, you know before you start that the dough will be sticky.

## How do you scale a recipe up or down?

The Ratio Calculator does it in one step per ingredient. Leave **Calculate** on **Missing value (A : B = C : D)**, put the old flour weight in **A**, the ingredient in **B**, the new flour weight in **C** and leave **D** empty. The tool solves it by cross-multiplication, A × D = B × C.

Scaling the loaf from 360 g to 500 g of flour:

- Water: 360 : 236.6 = 500 : D gives D = 328.611111111, so about 329 g.
- Salt: 360 : 9 = 500 : D gives 12.5 g.
- Yeast: 360 : 7 = 500 : D gives 9.72222222222, so about 9.7 g.

Under the answer you also get the whole proportion written out and the ratio as a decimal. Exactly one of A, B, C and D has to be blank; fill all four or leave two empty and the tool asks you to "Fill in three of A, B, C and D and leave one empty."

## How do you split the flour between two types?

Now replace part of the white flour with whole wheat. Switch **Calculate** to **Split a total in a ratio**, enter 4 in **A**, 1 in **B** and 500 in **Total to split**. The **Shares** come out as 400 and 100, and the tool confirms the ratio as 4 : 1 with a **Share of A** of 80 % and a **Share of B** of 20 %. So that's 400 g of bread flour and 100 g of whole wheat flour, still 500 g in total, so every other baker's percentage still applies.

The third mode, **Simplify a ratio**, helps when a recipe gives an awkward pair of weights. Enter 450 and 50 and it reduces them to 9 : 1, with a percent split of 90 % / 10 %.

## How much extra water does whole wheat flour need?

Whole wheat flour soaks up more water than white flour, so you'll usually add a little. Open the Percentage Increase Calculator and set **Calculation** to **Increase or decrease a value by a %**. Put the scaled water, 329, in **Original value** and 5 in **Percentage**. You get 345.45 as "329 increased by 5%", 312.55 as the decreased value, and 16.45 as the **Amount of change**. Weigh out about 345 g.

To check what that does to the hydration, go back to the Percentage Calculator with **X is what % of Y?**: 345.45 of 500 is 69.09%. Adding 5% to the water raises the hydration by less than 5 points, because the 5% is taken of the water, while hydration is measured against the flour. How much extra your flour needs depends on the flour, so treat 5% as a starting point and adjust on the next bake.

## How do you plan an overnight proof?

A cold rise in the fridge fits around a working day. Say you shape the loaf and put it in the fridge at 21:00 and take it out at 07:30. In the Time Duration Calculator, enter 21:00 as the **Start time** and 07:30 as the **End time**, and change **Break** from its default of 30 to 0, since nothing is being subtracted here. The **Duration** is 10 h 30 min, which is also 10.50 decimal hours and 630 total minutes. Because the end time is earlier than the start time, the tool adds a note that it has been treated as the next day.

If your recipe gives the dough 8 to 12 hours in the fridge, 10 h 30 min fits.

## FAQ

### Why do bakers use percentages instead of cups?

Percentages are tied to the flour weight, so they work at any batch size and with any unit. A cup of flour can weigh 120 g or 140 g depending on how it was filled, but 2.5% salt is always 2.5% of whatever flour you weighed.

### What is dough hydration?

It's the water weight as a percentage of the flour weight. In this example 236.6 g of water to 360 g of flour is 65.7222% hydration, and after the switch to whole wheat it's 69.09%.

### Does the converter know the weight of bread flour and yeast?

Yes. The ingredient list includes bread flour at 120 g per cup, whole wheat flour at 113 g, table salt at 288 g, Diamond Crystal kosher salt at 135 g and instant or active dry yeast at 149 g per cup. These are typical values from baking weight charts, and a kitchen scale is still the most accurate.
