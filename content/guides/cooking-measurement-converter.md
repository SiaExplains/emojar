When a converted recipe fails, the cause is almost always one of five things: the conversion ignored the ingredient, the cup or spoon came from a different country, the flour was scooped, the salt was a different type, or the oven temperature was meant for a fan oven. Each has a simple fix. Below, every problem comes with its symptom and a worked number showing how big the error is.

## Problem 1: Did you treat a cup as a fixed number of grams?

**Symptom:** some ingredients come out right and others are wildly off.

A cup is a volume, a gram is a weight, and the bridge between them is density, which differs for every ingredient. A single "1 cup = 240 g" rule works for water and milk and fails for almost everything else. Typical weights of one level US cup in the converter:

| Ingredient | Grams per US cup |
| --- | --- |
| Cocoa powder | 84 |
| Rolled oats | 89 |
| All-purpose flour | 120 |
| Granulated sugar | 198 |
| Butter | 227 |
| Water | 236.6 |
| Table salt | 288 |
| Honey | 336 |

Converting 2¼ cups of all-purpose flour gives **270 g**; treating it like water would give 532 g, nearly double.

**Fix:** always pick the ingredient. The converter's ingredient list shows each item's grams per cup right in the dropdown.

## Problem 2: Was the recipe written with a different cup?

**Symptom:** everything is slightly too dry or too wet, by roughly the same proportion.

The US cup is 236.6 ml, the metric cup (Australia, New Zealand, Canada) is 250 ml, and older British recipes may use an imperial cup of about 284 ml. One cup of all-purpose flour is about 120 g, 126.8 g or 144.1 g respectively, a 20% spread.

Spoons vary too: a US tablespoon is about 14.8 ml, a metric one 15 ml, and an Australian one 20 ml. Two Australian tablespoons of vinegar are about 2.7 US tablespoons (closest kitchen fraction: 2⅔).

**Fix:** check where the recipe comes from, then pick the matching unit in the **From** and **To** menus, such as "Cup (metric, AU/NZ/CA)" or "Tablespoon (Australia, 20 ml)".

## Problem 3: Did you scoop the flour?

**Symptom:** baked goods are dense, dry or crumbly even though the numbers looked right.

The 120 g figure is for flour spooned into the cup and leveled. Dipping the cup into the bag packs it down and can add 20% or more, roughly 140 g. Brown sugar is the opposite: recipes expect it packed, and the tool's 213 g assumes that.

**Fix:** assume the author spooned and leveled unless they say otherwise. If you must use cups, spoon flour in loosely and sweep the top flat. Better still, weigh it.

## Problem 4: Did you swap one salt for another?

**Symptom:** food is noticeably too salty or bland.

One US teaspoon of table salt weighs about **6 g**; a teaspoon of Diamond Crystal kosher salt about **2.8 g**. Swap them by volume and you've doubled or halved the salt.

**Fix:** select the exact salt in the ingredient list, or convert to grams and use a scale. Leaveners differ too: a teaspoon of baking soda is about 6 g, baking powder about 4 g.

## Problem 5: Was the oven setting meant for a fan oven?

**Symptom:** the outside browns too fast and the middle is underdone, or bakes run consistently early.

Many European recipes give a fan (convection) temperature, about 20 °C lower than a conventional oven. The converter's oven chart lists gas mark, °C, °C fan and °F side by side: gas mark 4 is 180 °C, 160 °C fan, or 350 °F.

**Fix:** find the recipe's temperature in its column and read across to your oven's. For any other temperature, use the [temperature converter](/online-tool/unit-converters/temperature-converter).

## When should you not trust a conversion?

Cup-to-gram figures are typical averages from published baking charts, not measurements of your bag of flour; brand, humidity and grind all shift the real weight. For bread, macarons or anything where 5% matters, weigh with a scale. And if a recipe was written in grams, keep it in grams.

## FAQ

### Why does a stick of butter show 7.993 tablespoons instead of 8?

A US stick is defined as 4 ounces of weight (113.4 g), while tablespoons are volume, so the converter goes through butter's density of 227 g per cup. The rounding produces 7.993, which shows as "≈ 8" next to the result. In practice, a stick is 8 tablespoons.

### How many grams is 1 teaspoon of instant yeast?

About 3.1 g. A standard 7 g packet is roughly 2¼ teaspoons, which the converter displays as "≈ 2 ¼" when you convert 7 g to teaspoons with yeast selected.

### Do I need the ingredient for cups to tablespoons?

No. Volume-to-volume and weight-to-weight conversions don't depend on density, so the ingredient is ignored. One US cup is always 16 tablespoons. For non-kitchen volume or weight units, try the [volume converter](/online-tool/unit-converters/volume-converter).
