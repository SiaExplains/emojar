Typing speed is measured in words per minute (WPM), and most adults land somewhere between 35 and 45. You can find your own number in one minute with the [Typing Speed Test](/online-tool/text-tools/typing-speed-test), and with ten to fifteen minutes of focused practice a day, most people gain 10–20 WPM within a month. The trick is to train accuracy first and let speed follow.

## What does WPM actually measure?

A "word" in a typing test is not a real word. It is any five characters, spaces and punctuation included. This standard makes scores comparable: typing "the cat sat" counts the same as typing a long technical term of the same length.

The test reports three numbers, and each tells you something different:

- **Net WPM** counts only the characters you typed correctly. It is the headline score and the one employers mean.
- **Raw WPM** counts everything you typed, mistakes included. A big gap between raw and net WPM means you are rushing.
- **Accuracy** is the share of key presses that were right, including mistakes you fixed with Backspace. Corrected errors still cost time, so they count here.

Here is a worked example. In a 30-second test you type 190 characters, and 180 of them are correct. Net WPM is 180 ÷ 5 = 36 words in half a minute, or 72 WPM. Raw WPM is 190 ÷ 5 × 2 = 76. If you pressed 200 keys in total and 12 of them were wrong, accuracy is 188 ÷ 200 = 94%.

## What is a good typing speed?

These ranges are a useful guide for a one-minute test on a physical keyboard:

| Net WPM | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Under 25 | Beginner, often still looking at the keys |
| 25–40 | Average for most adults |
| 40–60 | Above average and comfortable for office work |
| 60–80 | Fast, typical of professional typists |
| 80+ | Very fast, competitive typing territory |

Data-entry and transcription jobs often ask for 50–60 WPM with 95% accuracy or better. For everyday work, accuracy matters more than the last few words per minute. Fixing a typo takes longer than typing the word correctly the first time.

## How do you take a fair test?

A single result swings by several WPM depending on the text and how awake you are, so measure your baseline properly before you start practising:

1. Open the [Typing Speed Test](/online-tool/text-tools/typing-speed-test) and choose **1 min** with **Common words**. Leave punctuation and numbers off for the first test.
2. Take three tests in a row and note the middle result, not the best one. The tool keeps your last ten results and your best score for each length, all in your browser.
3. Repeat with **Sentences** mode. Real text has capitals and punctuation, so expect a slightly lower score, which is closer to how you actually type emails.

Short 15-second tests show your burst speed. Three- and five-minute tests show the pace you can keep up, which is usually a few WPM lower. If the gap is large, your accuracy probably drops as you tire.

## A four-week plan to type faster

This plan needs 10–15 minutes a day. Short daily sessions beat one long weekend session, because the skill is built by repetition and sleep.

**Week 1: posture and home row.** Sit with your forearms level, wrists floating rather than resting, and fingers curved over A S D F and J K L ;. Feel the bumps on F and J. Type slowly enough to keep accuracy at 97% or higher, even if your WPM drops for a few days. That dip is normal when you stop looking at the keys.

**Week 2: accuracy drills.** Take 30-second tests and aim for zero errors, not speed. When you make a mistake, notice which finger made it. Most errors come from a few keys, often the top row and the letters reached by the little fingers.

**Week 3: rhythm.** Try to type at an even pace instead of fast bursts followed by pauses. The chart after each test shows your pace second by second, and a flatter line means a steadier rhythm. Common words like "the", "and" and "that" should become single movements rather than three separate keys.

**Week 4: endurance and real text.** Switch to three-minute tests and Sentences mode with punctuation on. This is where the speed you built transfers to real writing.

At the end of the month, repeat your baseline: three one-minute tests, take the middle score. Compare it with week one.

## Common questions

**Should I learn a different keyboard layout?** For most people, no. Layouts like Dvorak or Colemak can be more comfortable, but switching costs weeks of slow typing. Good technique on QWERTY gets you well past 60 WPM.

**Does a better keyboard help?** A little. A keyboard with clear key travel can reduce errors, but practice matters far more than hardware. You can check that every key registers with the [Keyboard Tester](/online-tool/device-tests/keyboard-tester).

**Can I practise on my phone?** You can take the test there, but phone typing is a different skill with different numbers. Train on the keyboard you use for work.

Once your typing feels automatic, put it to use: draft longer pieces in the [Online Notepad](/online-tool/productivity-tools/online-notepad) and check their length with the [Word Counter](/online-tool/text-tools/word-counter).
