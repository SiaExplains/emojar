To work with tape-measure fractions, convert each measurement to a single fraction, put the fractions over a common denominator, do the arithmetic, then turn the answer back into inches and a fraction. A fraction calculator does all of that exactly, so 3 1/4" plus 2 5/8" comes out as 5 7/8" instead of a rounded decimal you then have to find on the tape.

Here is how that plays out on a real project: four bookshelf boards cut from one 8-foot plank.

## The project

You want four shelves, each 23 3/8" long, from a board that is 96" long. Your saw blade removes about 1/8" of wood with every cut (that strip is called the kerf). Will four shelves fit, and what is left over?

## Step 1: How long are the four shelves together?

Multiply one shelf by four: 23 3/8 × 4. In the [Fraction Calculator](/online-tool/calculators/fraction-calculator), type `23 3/8` as the first fraction, choose **× Multiply**, and type `4` as the second.

The result is **187/2**, with the mixed number **93 1/2** and the decimal 93.5. Open the **Step by step** table and you see why: 23 3/8 is rewritten as 187/8, the tops and bottoms are multiplied to give 748/8, and dividing both by 4 simplifies it to 187/2.

## Step 2: How much does the saw eat?

Four pieces mean four cuts, since the last cut separates the fourth shelf from the offcut. Four cuts at 1/8" each is 4 × 1/8 = **1/2**. That half inch is easy to forget.

## Step 3: What is left over?

Add the shelves and the kerf: 93 1/2 + 1/2 = **94**. Then subtract from the board: 96 − 94 = **2**. You have 2" to spare, enough to square up a rough end before you start.

Had you ignored the kerf, you would have planned on 2 1/2" of spare and been surprised.

## Step 4: How many pieces could the board give?

Suppose you also need small blocks 7 1/4" long. Divide 96 by 7 1/4: choose **÷ Divide** and type `96` and `7 1/4`. The calculator multiplies by the reciprocal (96 × 4/29) and returns **384/29**, or **13 7/29** as a mixed number and 13.2413793103 as a decimal. That means 13 whole blocks, before kerf. With 13 cuts at 1/8" you lose another 1 5/8", so check that the leftover still covers it.

## Step 5: Finding a center line

To drill a hole in the middle of a 48 1/2" panel, halve it: 48 1/2 ÷ 2 = **97/4**, which is **24 1/4**. Mark at 24 1/4" from either edge.

The last row is worth noticing. A negative result tells you the piece is 1/8" too short, which is often easier to read than comparing two mixed numbers by eye.

## How to type measurements correctly

The two input boxes, **First fraction** and **Second fraction**, are plain text fields, so the format matters:

- **Mixed numbers need a space**: `23 3/8`, not `23-3/8` or `233/8`.
- **A minus sign goes in front of the whole value**: `-2 3/8` means negative two and three-eighths. Writing `1 -1/2` is not accepted.
- **Decimals work** and are converted exactly: `0.375` becomes 3/8. That helps when a digital caliper shows decimals and the plans use fractions.
- **Whole numbers work** without a denominator: `96`.
- **The numerator and denominator must be whole numbers**: `1.5/2` is rejected. Type `0.75` or `3/4` instead.
- **Zero denominators are refused**, and dividing by zero shows "You can't divide by zero."

Because the math is done with whole numbers rather than floating-point decimals, the fraction result is exact no matter how many steps you chain together. Only the **Decimal** and **Percent** lines are rounded for display.

## Using the steps to check your own work

If you are learning fractions or helping a student, the step table is the most useful part. For 3 1/4 + 2 5/8 it shows three lines: the inputs written as 13/4 and 21/8, the common denominator 8 giving 26/8 + 21/8, and the sum 47/8. Trying the problem on paper first and then comparing each line points straight at the step where you went wrong.

## FAQ

### Why does the result show 187/2 when I expected a mixed number?

The main **Result** is always the simplified improper fraction. The **Mixed number** line right below it gives the tape-measure form, 93 1/2 in this case.

### Can I enter metric measurements?

Yes, as decimals. 12.5 cm is read as 25/2, and the arithmetic stays exact. Fractions are just less common with metric tools, so the decimal line is usually the one you will read.

### Does it round to the nearest sixteenth?

No. It gives the exact value, such as 13 7/29, rather than snapping to a tape-measure mark. To find the nearest sixteenth, multiply the leftover fraction by 16: 7/29 × 16 = 3 25/29, so it sits just under 4/16, or 1/4".
