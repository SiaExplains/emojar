To find a sale price, multiply the original price by (1 − discount ÷ 100): 25% off 120 is 120 × 0.75 = **90**. When a second discount is applied on top, such as "an extra 10% off sale prices", you multiply again rather than adding the percentages, so 25% plus 10% gives a total of **32.5% off**, not 35%. That one rule explains most of the confusion around sales.

Here is how to work out any discount, why stacking behaves the way it does, and a checklist to run before you buy.

## How do you calculate a discount?

Two quick formulas:

- **Savings = price × discount ÷ 100**
- **Sale price = price × (1 − discount ÷ 100)**

For a 120 jacket at 25% off: savings 30, sale price **90**.

## Why don't stacked discounts add up?

The second discount applies to the already-reduced price, not to the original. Take the same 120 jacket:

1. 25% off 120 → 90
2. An extra 10% off 90 → 9 off → **81**

You save 39 in total, which is 39 ÷ 120 = **32.5%** of the original price. If the discounts added up, you would pay 78 (35% off). The difference is small here, but it grows as the percentages grow.

The extreme case makes the point: **50% off, then an extra 50% off**, does not make an item free. An 80 item drops to 40, then to **20**: a total of 75% off.

The general formula for two discounts a and b is:

**Effective discount = 1 − (1 − a) × (1 − b)**

For 30% and 15%: 1 − 0.70 × 0.85 = 0.405, or **40.5% off**. Order doesn't matter; 10% then 25% gives exactly the same price as 25% then 10%.

## A sale shopping checklist

Before you decide a deal is a deal, run through these:

1. **Check the original price.** A discount off an inflated "was" price is not a saving. If you can, compare against what the item sold for a few weeks ago.
2. **Calculate the effective discount for stacked offers.** "30% off + extra 15%" on a 249.99 item is 148.74, saving 101.25, an effective **40.5%**, not 45%.
3. **Compare unit prices, not just percentages.** "Buy one, get the second half price" is 25% off when you buy two, and no discount at all if you only needed one.
4. **Factor in shipping and returns.** A cheaper item with a 9.99 shipping fee and no free returns may cost more than a smaller discount elsewhere.
5. **Know whether tax is added after the discount.** In most places, sales tax or VAT is charged on the discounted price. The [VAT Calculator](/online-tool/calculators/vat-calculator) can add it back if a price is shown without tax.
6. **Check the cap.** Some promo codes give "20% off up to 50", so the percentage stops applying after a certain basket size.

## Working backward: what was the original price?

If you know the sale price and the discount, divide instead of multiplying:

**Original price = sale price ÷ (1 − discount ÷ 100)**

A shirt on sale for 75 at 25% off originally cost 75 ÷ 0.75 = **100**. Subtracting 25% from 75 (to get 56.25) is a common mistake, because the 25% was taken from the original price, not the sale price.

## When percentage-off is the wrong comparison

Percentages help compare offers on the same item. They are less helpful across different items or stores. A 10% discount on something you were going to buy anyway saves real money; a 60% discount on something you didn't need costs you 40% of its price. ## FAQ

### Is "40% off" the same as "pay 60%"?

Yes. A 40% discount means you pay 60% of the original price. Thinking of it as "pay 60%" makes the final price quicker to estimate: 60% of 150 is 90.

### Does the calculator support three discounts?

It takes two: a main discount and one extra discount. For a third, run the final price from the first calculation back in as the new original price.

### Why does the effective discount show decimals?

Because combined discounts rarely land on whole numbers. 25% followed by 10% is exactly 32.5%, and the calculator shows it that way rather than rounding it.
